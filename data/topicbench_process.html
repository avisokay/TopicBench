Building the TopicBench corpus

Social-science papers that run classical topic models and publish author-labeled topic tables — pipeline, yields, and what the failures reveal about disciplinary reporting norms.

Pipeline

  1. Harvest. OpenAlex title_and_abstract.search for LDA / STM / topic-model phrases, 2008–2026, stratified across 19 discipline strata built from OpenAlex subfield IDs. Sorting by citation count within stratum stops the AI/ML subfields — which dominate the raw pool — from swamping the substantive social sciences.
  2. Screen. 371 abstracts classified by LLM against the inclusion criteria (must run a topic model; substantive social-science question; not methods-only; not end-to-end LLM labeling). A rule-based screen calibrated on those labels — precision 0.80, recall 0.92 — carried the remaining 1,033. Recall was favoured deliberately: false positives die at PDF verification, false negatives are unrecoverable.
  3. Acquire. Europe PMC JATS XML (132 papers) plus PDFs retrieved through institutional access and exported from Zotero, including 15 purposive additions. Publisher domains are unreachable from an automated client and bot-protected, so human-in-the-loop retrieval was the only legitimate route.
  4. Extract. Three routes, escalating in cost.

How the corpus was built, in order

The sequence matters for reading the table above, because the corpus is two samples with different selection processes.

  1. Stratified harvest first. 19 discipline strata, screened and extracted without reference to any hypothesis about labeling. The CS-tradition gap (7/32 = 22% versus 132/262 = 50%, Fisher OR 0.28, p = 0.0024) emerged from this pass. It was a finding about the sample, not a target of it.
  2. Late full-text retrieval, not new sampling. The first pass could only obtain full text for part of the harvest — publisher paywalls, not screening, were the binding constraint. A later pass used institutional credentials to retrieve 68 further PDFs from that same stratified draw (verified: 68 of 70 appear in the original 1,404-candidate pool). These are not a new sample and carry no selection on the outcome; they simply arrived late. They are pooled with the harvest for every prevalence estimate below.
  3. Purposive augmentation, 15 papers. Separately, 15 papers absent from the harvest were added from domain familiarity because they were known to publish topic tables. These are selected on the outcome and are excluded from every prevalence estimate; they remain in the benchmark as evaluation items.
Assembling the CS-tradition portion of the benchmark required hand-picking papers from memory of the literature, because random sampling within the stratum kept returning papers without topic labels. Two measurements size that difficulty:

  • The stratified pass screened 4.6 CS-tradition full texts per usable benchmark item, against 2.0 elsewhere — 2.3× the search cost.
  • 46% of the CS-tradition labeled topics in the benchmark (53 of 115) come from the 15 purposive papers, so in these venues the stratified draw alone leaves the benchmark thin.
This reveals a difference in epistemology across fields in the sampled papers that employ classical topic modeling. Where the topic model is an instrument inside a larger pipeline, the topic table is intermediate output and labeled tables are scarce enough that assembling a benchmark means going and finding them. Where the model is the object of interpretation, the labeled table is the publishable claim and appears as a matter of course. That scarcity is the motivation for TopicBench: a benchmark of human topic labeling has to reach past the fields that publish labels routinely.

The mechanism shows in the failure notes. Across papers where no usable table was found, the largest category is an unlabeled topic table — the model was run, top terms were printed, and no interpretive label was ever assigned. Among CS-adjacent papers this reaches 46%. A further 17% state labels only in prose (“Topic 3, which we interpret as institutional trust”) without tabulating them.

The pattern is consistent with a difference in what the topic model is for. Where the model is a measurement instrument — a feature for a downstream classifier, a coherence benchmark, a retrieval component — naming the topics serves no purpose in the argument. Where topics are the finding, as in interpretive work, labeling is the analysis. Anthropology at 88% and Information Science at 19% are two ends of that distinction, not a difference in rigour.

Implication for TopicBench. The benchmark's ground truth is drawn disproportionately from interpretive traditions, because those are the papers that produce labels at all. That is a property of the construct. Evaluation results describe how researchers in label-publishing traditions name topics; reporting per-discipline performance keeps that scope visible.

Secondary observations

  • Median 2-word labels (mean 2.8, max 23); 76% are 1–3 words. Median 10 keywords per cluster (range 2–47).
  • Sentence-like labels are rare — 26 of 2,290 (1.1%). A naïve length filter (>6 words) would discard 116 rows to catch them, most of which are valid long noun phrases.
  • 301 labels (13%) carried a table row number (“25 GM Food”) that had to be stripped — it is table furniture an AI labeler never sees.
  • Full-text access, not screening, was the binding constraint. 839 papers passed screening; 396 full texts were obtainable.

Reporting practice: the keyword–label pair is the scarce object

Across the whole project we read 396 papers in full, of which 360 apply an LDA/STM-family topic model to a substantive research question; the remaining 36 are methods, toolkit, survey or bibliometric papers with no empirical topic model to interpret.

Labels and keywords are each reported reasonably often. What is rare is the two together, in a form a reader can align. Of the 142 papers read in full that yielded no usable table, 106 genuinely apply a topic model (the rest are methods, toolkit, survey or bibliometric papers). Those 106 break down as:

More papers are unusable for want of keywords than for want of labels — 59 versus 33, a factor of 1.8. The interpretive work is usually done and published; what goes missing is the machine output it was derived from. A paper reporting “Topic 26 (pharmaceutical ingredient chemistry)” with the top terms nowhere in the article or its appendix has published a conclusion without the evidence, and is unusable for validating any labeling method — human or model.

Reporting completeness travels together

Papers that publish the pair also tend to explain how the labels were produced, and the gradient is steep:

Paper reports…nDescribes its labeling process
paired (extracted)119 88%
labels but no keywords59 66%
keywords but no label33 12%

This is not a story about carelessness. A paper that prints top terms and never names the topics has nothing to describe — the 3 of 26 figure for that row is close to what it should be. The informative row is the middle one: papers that did the interpretive work and named their topics, yet documented the procedure only 59% of the time and withheld the terms those names were read off.

Most process descriptions are a single sentence. Among the 105 papers that say something about labeling, only 26 (25%) mention multiple coders, independent labeling, or reconciliation. The rest are single sentences of the form “we examined common themes and ideas presented within each topic” — sufficient to confirm human involvement, insufficient to assess reliability or reproduce the procedure.

Suggested reporting minimum

Four items, none of which needs main-text space. Each is already satisfied by some papers in this corpus.

  1. Publish the top terms next to the label, in one table. A separate appendix table keyed by a topic number that appears nowhere else does not suffice. Alignment is what makes the pair usable; 27 papers here broke it.
  2. Tabulate, don't narrate. 32 papers name their topics only in running text. Prose labels cannot be extracted reliably at scale and are frequently ambiguous about which topic they refer to.
  3. State how many terms you are showing, and how they were ranked — highest probability, FREX, lift. Cluster sizes in this corpus range from 2 to 47 terms with no stated convention, and word-cloud figures encode rank in font size only approximately.
  4. Describe the labeling procedure in two or three sentences: who labeled, whether independently, how disagreements were resolved, and what they saw beyond the top terms (representative documents, metadata, domain knowledge). This last point matters most for benchmarking — a keyword-only prompt to a model is not the task the humans performed, and only a paper that says what its labelers saw lets anyone construct a fair comparison.

An appendix table and one methods paragraph is the whole ask. It costs a page and makes the paper's central interpretive claim checkable.

Why this matters beyond replication

Topic labels are a claim about what a corpus is about, made by people, from evidence. When the evidence is withheld the claim cannot be audited by a replicator, a reviewer, or anyone trying to establish whether an automated labeler could stand in for a human. Every paper in the 59-paper group above did the hard interpretive work and then made it unverifiable at the cost of one table.

Prompt 1 — abstract inclusion screen LLM

Applied to 371 of 1,404 candidates; the deterministic screen calibrated against its output handled the rest. Returned through a forced tool call requiring include, algorithms, domain, corpus, topic_model_role (8-value enum), confidence and reason.

You screen scholarly records for a benchmark of social-science papers that APPLY classical topic modeling and REPORT author-written topic labels alongside keyword lists. Decide from title + abstract + venue.

INCLUDE requires ALL of: (a) an EMPIRICAL study that actually RUNS a classical topic model (LDA, STM, CTM, DTM, NMF, LSA, BERTopic, Top2Vec) on a text corpus; (b) a substantive social-science / humanities / public-health / management / communication / HCI / education / policy research question; (c) the model is used to interpret latent themes, so the authors would plausibly present a table of topics with keywords and human-assigned labels.

EXCLUDE: methods-only or algorithmic contributions; bibliometric or scientometric mapping of a research literature; reviews/surveys/tutorials ABOUT topic modeling; papers that only cite it without running it; end-to-end LLM topic modeling with LLM-generated labels (TopicGPT, LLooM); engineering/biomedical/CV applications with no social-science question. Missing or too-vague abstract -> include=false, confidence='low'.

Prompt 2 — topic-table extraction from page images vision

Governs the vision and vision_local routes (90 of 178 papers). Full text in EXTRACTION_PROMPT.md; the paramount instruction is the alignment invariant:

len(clusters) == len(author_labels), and index i in one corresponds to
index i in the other. THIS ALIGNMENT IS THE MOST IMPORTANT INVARIANT.
A row where topic 3's label sits beside topic 4's keywords is worse
than no row at all.

The same prompt disqualifies LLM-generated labels (the benchmark compares models against humans, so a paper whose labels came from a model cannot be ground truth), rejects representative quotes mistaken for keywords, requires verbatim transcription with no tidying, and requires a record for every paper including failures — which is what makes the reporting-practice denominators below complete rather than survivorship-filtered.

Prompt 3 — classifying why a paper yielded no table LLM

Analysis-only; drives the reporting-practice section. Forced tool call with a fixed 7-value enum plus a confidence rating.

You classify short extraction notes written by an analyst who searched a paper's
full text for a table pairing topic-model KEYWORD CLUSTERS with AUTHOR-WRITTEN TOPIC LABELS,
and did not find a usable one. Assign exactly ONE primary reason, via the tool.

Categories (choose the single best fit):
 - labels_only: the paper DOES run a topic model and DOES give author-written topic
   labels/names/themes, but never prints the top terms / keywords for the topics.
 - keywords_only: the paper prints top terms / keyword lists but assigns NO interpretive
   author label (bare "Topic 1", numbers, or probabilities only).
 - labels_in_prose_only: labels exist but only in running text or as prose descriptions,
   never tabulated next to keywords.
 - both_missing: runs a topic model but reports neither a keyword table nor labels.
 - not_an_application: no empirical topic model interpreted at all — methods/algorithm
   paper, software or toolkit demo, survey/review, or bibliometric literature mapping.
 - llm_generated: the topic labels were produced by an LLM/automatic labeler, not humans.
 - unclear: the note does not say enough to tell.

Judge ONLY from the note text. If the note says the paper is a method/toolkit/survey paper
with no interpreted topic model, that is not_an_application even if it mentions tables.
What is not verified. The labeling-process yes/no and its quote are model readings that were not re-checked against the PDFs. corpus and method were corrected in a second pass but only where the first pass had flagged uncertainty. cited_by is a moving quantity. Everything marked rule is reproducible from the shipped CSVs by re-running the documented expression.

Validation

Extractions were checked against source documents by measuring what fraction of claimed keywords actually occur in the text: median grounding 1.00, mean 0.95. Low-scoring cases are all vision extractions from rasterized tables, where the terms are legitimately absent from the text layer; these were confirmed by rendering the page. Cluster/label positional alignment is asserted on all 178 rows, and the 15 purposive extractions were verified to round-trip against the local extraction output.

Vision takes precedence when routes conflict. One paper extracted as 44 topics by text-layer parsing and 15 by vision. Vision was right: the text-layer pass had split wrapped labels across rows and captured a table header as a topic. Row count does not indicate extraction quality.

Three defects are preserved rather than silently corrected: a mislabeled panel in a published figure (source_row_defect, tier excluded), word-cloud clusters whose keyword order is font-size-derived and only approximate (keyword_order_unreliable, 112 topics), and a replication whose labels were written by the study it replicates (labels_borrowed_from_prior_study).
