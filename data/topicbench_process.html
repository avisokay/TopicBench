Building the TopicBench corpus

Stratified harvest of social-science papers that run classical topic models and publish author-labeled topic tables — pipeline, yields, and what the failures revealed about disciplinary reporting norms.

Pipeline

  1. Harvest. OpenAlex title_and_abstract.search for LDA / STM / topic-model phrases, 2008–2026, stratified across 19 discipline strata built from OpenAlex subfield IDs. Sorting by citation count within stratum prevents the AI/ML subfields — which dominate the raw pool — from swamping the substantive social sciences.
  2. Screen. 371 abstracts classified by LLM against the TopicBench criteria (must run a topic model; substantive social-science question; not methods-only; not end-to-end LLM labeling). A rule-based screen calibrated on those labels — precision 0.80, recall 0.92 — carried the remaining 1,033. Recall was favoured deliberately: false positives die at PDF verification, false negatives are unrecoverable.
  3. Acquire. Europe PMC JATS XML where available (132 papers); the rest via the author's institutional access, exported from a Zotero collection (183 PDFs). Publisher domains are unreachable from the sandbox and bot-protected against non-browser clients, so human-in-the-loop retrieval was the only legitimate route.
  4. Extract. Three routes, escalating in cost.

What we learned: labeling is a disciplinary norm, not a technical one

Of 309 papers with full text, only 113 (37%) published a topic table pairing keyword clusters with author-written labels. The other 63% is the substantive finding — and the omission is not distributed evenly across fields.

The dominant category is an unlabeled topic table — the model was run, the top terms were printed, and no interpretive label was ever assigned. Among CS-adjacent papers this rises to 46%. A second 17% state labels only in prose (“Topic 3, which we interpret as institutional trust”) without tabulating them.

The pattern is consistent with a difference in what the topic model is for. Where the model is a measurement instrument — a feature for a downstream classifier, a coherence benchmark, a retrieval component — naming the topics serves no purpose in the argument. Where topics are the finding, as in interpretive work, labeling is the analysis. Anthropology at 88% and Information Science at 18% are two ends of that distinction, not a difference in rigour.

Implication for TopicBench. The benchmark's ground truth is drawn disproportionately from interpretive traditions, because those are the papers that produce labels at all. That is a property of the construct, not a sampling defect — but evaluation results should not be read as covering how all social scientists would label a topic. Reporting per-discipline performance is the honest presentation.

Secondary observations

  • Median k = 10 topics per labeled table (range 3–70). Communication runs highest (median 20), reflecting large news and social-media corpora.
  • 60 of 68 papers that discuss their labeling procedure describe a human, often multi-coder process — independent labeling then reconciliation. Verbatim quotes are captured in labeling_process_quote.
  • Full-text access, not screening, was the binding constraint. 839 papers passed screening; 309 full texts were obtainable.

Validation

Every extracted record was checked against its source PDF by measuring what fraction of claimed keywords actually occur in the document: median grounding 1.00, mean 0.95. Low-scoring cases are all vision-extracted from rasterized tables, where the terms are legitimately absent from the text layer; these were confirmed by rendering the page. Cluster/label positional alignment is asserted on all 131 rows.

Row count is not a quality signal. One paper extracted as 44 topics by text-layer parsing and 15 by vision. Vision was right: the text-layer pass had split wrapped labels across rows and captured a table header as a topic. Vision now takes precedence on conflict.
